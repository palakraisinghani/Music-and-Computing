section 4:
addressing the debugging problem with sound. electric transducers.
Watching bulbs and noting down values was tiresome, needed more labour and an overkill for most simple debugging cases, like finding out of the program has halted, entered an infinite loop, is stalling, etc.
This problem was solved by introducing hooters. These were electricity to sound transducers. They were of two kinds: Passive Sonification and Active Audio.
Passive sonification refered to hardware eavesdropping by connecting small electrical components on a wire that sensed voltage switches and converted the rate of voltage change to sound. Active audio referred to having special instructions which specifically signalled the transducer. the frequency of these signals was converted to sound of diferent pitches. Now a person could just walk into the room and identify the stage of progress made by the program. Debugging was also made possible and reduced the amount of labour needed immensely.

Analog Sampling Came First (1948)
If sampling is defined broadly as capturing real-world acoustic sound from the physical environment and manipulating it as a musical instrument, sampling predates digital computing music by nearly a decade:
Musique Concrète (1948): Pierre Schaeffer and Pierre Henry in Paris recorded trains, voices, and instruments onto shellac discs and magnetic tape, physically slicing, splicing, looping, and transposing the playback speed.
The Chamberlin (1949) & Mellotron (1963): These electro-mechanical instruments placed pre-recorded analog tape strips underneath individual keyboard keys, functioning as purely mechanical analog samplers.
2. Digitally Synthesized Music Came Next (1957)
The generation of musical waveforms directly via digital computer algorithms emerged before computers were capable of digitizing and recording external analog audio:
Max Mathews & Newman Guttman (May 1957): At Bell Labs, Guttman composed "In the Silver Scale" using Mathews’s MUSIC I program on an IBM 704 mainframe.
The sound was synthesized entirely from mathematical functions (a triangle-wave algorithm) calculated sample-by-sample in software and output through a Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC). There was no recording or digitization of an existing acoustic instrument.
3. Digital Sampling Came Last (1969–1979)
Digital sampling—taking a microphone, running the acoustic signal through an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) into digital computer memory (RAM), and mapping it across a keyboard—lagged behind digital synthesis by over a decade.
The Hardware Bottleneck: Digitally synthesizing a tone only required a computer to compute numbers and push them through a DAC (Digital-to-Analog Converter), which could be done offline at a slow pace onto magnetic tape. Capturing a live external sound required a high-speed, linear ADC paired with large amounts of fast, random-access semiconductor memory (RAM) to store the sample in real time—hardware that was prohibitively expensive and technically immature in the 1950s and 1960s.
EMS Musys (1969–1972): Peter Zinovieff achieved the earliest experimental digital sampling using dual DEC PDP-8 minicomputers equipped with early ADCs to digitize live sounds into core memory.
Commercial Digital Samplers (1976–1979): Dedicated digital musical samplers only arrived with the Computer Music Melodian (1976) and reached widespread commercial maturity with the Fairlight CMI (1979) and E-mu Emulator (1981).
